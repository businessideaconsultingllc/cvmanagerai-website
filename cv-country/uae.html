The Middle East Advantage

Applying for jobs in Dubai, Abu Dhabi, or the wider GCC means competing in one of the most international talent pools anywhere — a shortlist for a single role can include candidates from a dozen countries. That has two consequences for your CV: it needs to communicate clearly to a reader who may not share your professional background, and it usually needs to include a few details that would be actively discouraged in the US or UK.

What UAE Employers Expect

Nationality and visa status. This is the biggest departure from Western norms. Employers deal with visa sponsorship constantly, so stating your nationality and current visa status (employment visa, residence visa, or requiring sponsorship) answers a practical question they'd otherwise have to ask.
A photo is common and generally fine. Unlike the US, a professional headshot is normal on a UAE CV and won't count against you. It isn't mandatory either — if you'd rather not, that's not a red flag.
Languages, listed with real proficiency levels. In a market where teams are routinely multilingual, "Arabic — native, English — fluent, Hindi — conversational" is genuinely useful information, not filler.
English is the default business language. Most multinational and private-sector roles run in English. Arabic is an advantage — sometimes a requirement for government or public-sector roles — but an English CV is the safe starting point unless the posting says otherwise.
2 pages is acceptable. The strict one-page US convention doesn't apply. Senior professionals routinely run to two, particularly where international experience needs context.

Key Hiring Sectors

Tailoring your keywords to the sector you're targeting matters more here than in a single-industry market:

  • Construction & real estate — project scale, budgets managed, delivery timelines
  • Finance & banking — regulatory frameworks, certifications, DIFC/ADGM experience
  • Hospitality & tourism — property size, guest volumes, service standards
  • Logistics & aviation — throughput figures, safety records, systems used
  • Healthcare — DHA, HAAD, or MOH licensing status is often screened first

Common Mistakes

Omitting visa status entirely. Recruiters need it to assess cost and timeline. Leaving it out doesn't help you — it just adds a step before anyone can shortlist you.
Sending an unadapted US resume. A one-page, no-personal-details document reads as thin to a UAE reader who expects nationality, languages, and fuller context.
Not naming your licensing status. In healthcare, engineering, and finance, professional licensing is often the first filter — put it near the top, not buried in a footer.
